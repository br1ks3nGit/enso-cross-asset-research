The December ENSO signal raises cooling electricity use, but it does not create a credible 2026 chiller-order wave

For the 4.576 GW of affected-state capacity separately disclosed in the source data, the central estimate of new cooling equipment capex attributable only to this ENSO forecast is $0. A deliberately conservative post-audit contingency screen is about $5.8 million, with a $2.3–$28.1 million scenario range. This is tiny beside the roughly $60 billion cooling-equipment basis associated with the already-announced 2025–30 capacity pipeline in the same six states.

Investment conclusion. The structural AI/data-center cooling theme is real; the September 2026 ENSO forecast is not a material incremental revenue catalyst for public cooling suppliers. Orders are already being placed for multi-year capacity buildouts, the relevant December sites remain cold, and the signal-to-season window is only about 12–16 weeks versus a reported 33-week average equipment lead time. Treat VRT, MOD, AAON, NVT, JCI or TT as structural infrastructure exposures—not as a clean one-season ENSO trade.

What would appear on the balance sheet?

New chillers, CDUs, pumps, fan walls and heat-rejection equipment are normally capitalized by a data-center owner as construction in progress and then transferred to property, plant and equipment when placed in service. Cash paid is capital expenditure in investing cash flow; depreciation reaches the income statement later. The $9.19 million central December electricity estimate from the earlier report is different: it is operating expense, not equipment capex.

The $0 central capex figure means no additional identifiable PP&E caused by this weather forecast. It does not mean operators are spending nothing on cooling. The six states' published capacity pipeline rises from 4.576 GW in 2025 to 28.219 GW in 2030; using the cost screen below, that expansion embeds about $60.0 billion of cooling equipment and infrastructure. That is planned growth capex and must not be relabeled “El Niño capex.” Capacity figures come from Lau & Tsai (2026), Table 1; the paper itself notes that disclosed/announced capacity is incomplete.

Why the modeled +2°C to +4°C does not imply more installed capacity

The prior forecast estimates an ENSO-associated contribution, not a forecast of the entire temperature departure. Joining it to NOAA state temperature data shows that the implied 1991–2020-normal-plus-ENSO December state means range from about −5.1°C in North Dakota to +2.7°C in Ohio. Even at the forecast effect's p90, every selected-state monthly mean remains below +4.4°C. Those temperatures reduce economizer/free-cooling hours and raise auxiliary electricity; they do not test the summer outdoor-design condition that determines installed heat-rejection capacity.

ASHRAE's AI data-center framework recommends climate-zone economization, modular liquid-cooling systems and N+1 or 2N pumping/heat-exchange redundancy. That engineering architecture is why a warm winter month normally consumes more energy before it triggers emergency equipment procurement. The original state effects and limitations remain in the September–December ENSO forecast.

How the contingency screen is calculated

This screen answers a narrower question: if a post-season engineering audit nevertheless finds small deficiencies, how much balance-sheet capex might be touched?

Cooling replacement basis per MW = build cost per MW × cooling share

Audit contingency capex = affected MW × replacement basis × incidence × scope

  • Build cost: $12m / $13m / $14m per MW. JLL's 2026 report gives $12m–$14m/MW for a Chicago 50 MW air-cooled shell-and-core facility and notes a 10% liquid-cooling premium.
  • Cooling share: 17.1% / 19.5% / 22.0%, calculated from AAON's June 2026 investor presentation: $7bn–$9bn North American cooling equipment/infrastructure TAM divided by about $41bn of data-center construction put in place.
  • Audit incidence: 0.5% / 1% / 2% of affected capacity; scoped addition: 5% / 5% / 10% of a complete cooling system. These are explicit analyst assumptions, not measured fleet failure rates.

The resulting central replacement basis is $2.54 million per MW. The contingency is $127 thousand per 100 MW, before applying a known site-specific deficiency. Since no breach is identified, the contingency is not the central forecast and should not be booked as committed capex.

Revenue alteration caused by this ENSO forecast

Central case

$0 incremental supplier revenue. With no design-capacity breach and insufficient procurement time, weather-attributable equipment capex is $0, so supplier revenue uplift = $0 × win share = $0. Companies still grow from AI/data-center construction, but that is the baseline structural theme.

Contingency case

If the full $5.8m audit screen were actually ordered, a supplier capturing 10%, 25% or 50% would receive only $0.58m, $1.45m or $2.90m. Even the entire screen is just 0.145% of Modine's $4bn agreement, 0.290% of AAON's total backlog and 0.039% of Vertiv's year-end backlog.

Have operators already adapted?

Substantially, yes—because of AI density, water and efficiency constraints, not this ENSO forecast. Microsoft says all new designs beginning in August 2024 adopted closed-loop, zero-water-for-cooling technology. Meta says most of its newest AI-optimized centers use closed-loop liquid cooling and describes air-assisted liquid cooling for older facilities. Google reports trailing-twelve-month PUE of 1.05–1.11 at its cited Ohio and Iowa campuses, which indicates highly optimized existing cooling operations.

Timing is also adverse for a September weather trade. JLL reports a 33-week average data-center equipment lead time, while the forecast-to-December window is only about 12–16 weeks. Large equipment serving December 2026 was therefore almost certainly specified or ordered before this signal.

Market test: was the cooling theme already in prices?

The equal-weight seven-OEM basket returned +22.6% from the first 2026 close to 11 September, versus +9.1% for XLI. But from 1 July to the signal date the basket lost 13.6%, worse than XLI's 6.0% decline. Decision rule

  • Do not trade this as September-forecast → December cooling-equipment revenue. The central incremental capex/revenue mechanism is absent.
  • Monitor operational data instead: local hourly dry-bulb/wet-bulb temperatures, economizer hours, PUE, cooling alarms, rental cooling, and emergency purchase orders. State monthly mean ΔT is too coarse.
  • For structural exposure, separate the thesis: new AI capacity, liquid-cooling adoption, order growth, backlog conversion, manufacturing yield and valuation—not ENSO.
  • Revisit only if a facility-level forecast crosses design conditions, a repeat multi-season warming pattern changes engineering standards, or operators disclose weather-driven retrofit orders.
